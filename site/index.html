How I work

Good software owes a lot to everyday habits. These are the ones I try to keep, and where you can see them in practice.

  1. 01

    Explain it plainly

    I'm used to explaining technical problems, and their fixes, to engineers and to colleagues who aren't technical. I write the same way: documentation and write-ups aimed at the person who wasn't in the room.

    Parity's design notes ↗
  2. 02

    Leave things easy to pick up

    When I pass a problem on, it goes with clear notes, so nobody has to start again from scratch. I've worked to professional engineering standards, where accurate records, agreed procedures and deadlines matter.

  3. 03

    Check before trusting

    I'd rather prove something than assume it. My backtester only fills trades at prices that really traded, and the live bot runs the backtester's own decision code, so any difference between the two shows up as a bug to fix. Hundreds of automated tests back them both.

    The parity loop →
  4. 04

    Fix the cause, not the symptom

    When my live bot once booked a winning exit that the broker hadn't actually filled, I traced the whole sequence, wrote it up, and changed how every exit settles instead of patching that one case.

    The 88-second phantom →
  5. 05

    Listen first

    Years of customer-facing work, from telesales to retail, taught me to listen properly and ask good questions before reaching for a fix. It served me just as well in IT support: understand the problem first, then solve it.

  6. 06

    Keep steady

    I've studied for my degree part-time over four years, alongside full-time work. It taught me to plan ahead, manage my time carefully and keep long pieces of work moving.

Computer science, from first principles

Alongside the degree I worked through an intensive introduction to computer science, built around weekly problem sets and a final project of my own:

  • C and memory: pointers, allocation and how data is laid out.
  • Algorithms and their cost: searching, sorting, recursion and Big O.
  • Data structures: linked lists, hash tables, trees and tries.
  • Python, SQL and the web: HTML, CSS, JavaScript and Flask.

The habits stuck too: correctness first, then design, then style. Test the edge cases, not just the happy path. And when I'm stuck, explain the problem out loud, even if it's only to a rubber duck.
